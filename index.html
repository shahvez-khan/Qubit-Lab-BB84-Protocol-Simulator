THE QUANTUM IDEA

Why does eavesdropping show?

02 / 03

Measuring an unknown quantum state can change it. BB84 turns that disturbance into a way to detect an eavesdropper.

Alice encodes each bit using one of two incompatible bases. Bob independently chooses a basis to measure each arriving qubit. After transmission they announce their bases over a public channel and keep only positions where their choices matched.

An interceptor who guesses the wrong basis gets a random result and resends a changed state. That disturbance raises the error rate in the sifted key. Alice and Bob reveal a random sample to estimate the QBER, then abort if it is too high.

01 / NO-CLONING

Unknown states can’t be copied

Quantum mechanics prevents an interceptor from making a perfect copy of an arbitrary unknown qubit before measuring it.

02 / BASIS MISMATCH

Half the bits are discarded

With random independent basis choices, Alice and Bob match about half the time. Only those positions form the sifted key.

03 / QBER

Errors reveal disturbance

Intercept-resend Eve creates about 25% errors in the sifted key when she attacks every qubit and chooses a random basis.

About this model

This is an educational, idealized BB84 simulation. Its bit-flip channel and intercept-resend Eve model illustrate the protocol, but do not represent a deployed quantum link. The displayed final key length is a simplified privacy-amplification estimate; production QKD requires finite-key security analysis, authentication, error correction, and a secure universal hash implementation.
